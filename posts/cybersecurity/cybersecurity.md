Cybersecurity isn't about building an impenetrable wall. It's about knowing when someone is trying to get through it.

And a supporting subheadline:

We help organizations detect unusual behavior, protect critical data, and respond to threats before a security incident becomes a business crisis.

A strong cybersecurity story for a website is one that starts with a relatable business problem rather than technical jargon. Given your technology/business background, I'd make it a short story about how a seemingly harmless event turns into a cybersecurity problem—and how good security prevents it.

Story concept: “The $2 Million Email”

It started with an email that looked completely normal.

Imagine this: It’s 9:17 on a Tuesday morning…, the CFO of a manufacturing company received an email from the CEO:

“We just closed the deal. Please wire the $2 million payment to the account below. I’m in meetings all day, so please handle this immediately.”

The email looked right. The CEO's name was correct. His signature was correct. The request even made sense—the company had just completed a major acquisition.

The CFO clicked Reply.

That's when something didn't feel right.

Instead of responding to the email, she called the CEO.

“Did you just ask me to wire $2 million?”

“No.”

The company had just avoided a catastrophic loss.

What happened next was more interesting. The security team discovered that an employee's credentials had been compromised several days earlier. The attacker had been quietly monitoring email conversations, learning how the company communicated, understanding its approval processes, and waiting for the right opportunity.

The attack wasn't really about technology.

It was about trust.

The attacker understood that people are trained to trust familiar names, familiar processes and familiar requests.

That's the challenge of cybersecurity today.

Firewalls and antivirus software are important. But modern cybersecurity is also about understanding who is doing what, when they're doing it, and whether their behavior makes sense.

The best security systems don't simply ask:

“Is this user authorized?”

They ask:

“Does this behavior make sense?”

And sometimes, the most important security technology isn't the thing that stops the attack.

It's the thing that gives someone a reason to stop and ask one more question.

The underlying cybersecurity scenario is realistic:

Business Email Compromise (BEC) attacks commonly involve compromised email accounts or impersonation.
Attackers can monitor communications and use information about a company's relationships and processes to make fraudulent requests appear credible.
Fraudulent wire-transfer requests are a well-established BEC technique.
Independent verification—such as calling the purported sender using a known phone number—is a recognized defense.

The real story: MGM Resorts, September 2023

MGM Resorts disclosed that in September 2023, criminal actors obtained unauthorized access to some of its U.S. systems. MGM responded by shutting down portions of its systems to contain the incident. The shutdown disrupted operations at its properties, including guest-facing systems, and MGM later reported that customer information had been obtained.

Contemporary reporting attributed the initial compromise to social engineering involving the company's help desk. That detail is useful because it illustrates an important point: sophisticated cybersecurity incidents don't necessarily begin with a sophisticated piece of malware.

MGM subsequently reported that the incident affected operations and contributed approximately $100 million of impact to its third-quarter results.

Here's how to tell the story:

**When the Casino Went Dark**

Imagine arriving at one of the world's most famous casinos.

The lights are on. The hotels are full. Thousands of guests are arriving, checking into rooms, making reservations and sitting down at gaming tables.

Then the technology starts to fail.

In September 2023, MGM Resorts experienced a cybersecurity incident that disrupted systems across its properties. MGM shut down certain systems as it investigated and worked to contain the attack. The disruption affected operations and guest-facing technology, and the company later confirmed that personal information belonging to some customers had been obtained.

The reported attack illustrates something important about modern cybersecurity: **an attack doesn't have to destroy your data to disrupt your business.**

Systems may have to be taken offline to contain an intrusion.

Employees may suddenly lose access to critical applications.

Customers can be affected.

Revenue can be lost.

And the security team can find itself racing against the clock to determine what happened, what was compromised and how to safely restore operations.

Reports about the MGM incident indicated that social engineering played a role in gaining access.

That is the uncomfortable reality of cybersecurity today.

The threat isn't always a sophisticated hacker breaking through a technological barrier.

Sometimes, it starts with **a person**.

A phone call.

A convincing request.

A compromised identity.

And one successful interaction with an employee.

Cybersecurity therefore has to be more than firewalls and passwords.

Organizations need to understand identities, monitor behavior, protect critical systems and data, detect unusual activity and respond quickly when something doesn't look right.

Because the goal isn't simply to prevent every attack.

**The goal is to make sure that when an attacker gets an opportunity, your business doesn't have to go dark.**

Why this is stronger than the fictional $2 million story

You can legitimately say that this is based on a real, publicly documented incident, rather than creating a fictional customer story.

You also have excellent sources to cite:

MGM's SEC filing describing the cybersecurity incident
MGM's September 2023 SEC disclosure
MGM's October 2023 incident update
Reuters' reporting on the operational and financial impact
